How to Write a Rent Increase Letter: Templates, Rules, and Landlord Best Practices

Property · RentFlow · 18 min read · Updated Published: Jul 22, 2026 · Updated: Jul 22, 2026

⚡ 30-second summary

Writing a professional rent increase notice requires clear mathematical justification, adherence to local state notice periods, and a polite but firm tone. Landlords must include specific details like the exact dollar increase, the effective date, and instructions for tenant acceptance. By using a structured template and calculating your cash-flow adjustments carefully, you can raise the rent without damaging your relationship with reliable tenants.

Managing rental properties requires a delicate balance between maintaining a profitable business and keeping dependable tenants in place. As inflation, property taxes, insurance premiums, and maintenance costs rise, landlords eventually face the necessity of raising the rent. However, communicating this change can be stressful. Knowing how to write a rent increase letter professionally is the most effective way to protect your cash flow while minimizing tenant turnover.

Key Takeaways

  • Check Local Rent Control Laws First: Always verify state and municipal statutes regarding maximum allowable increases and mandatory notice periods before drafting your letter.
  • Provide 30 to 90 Days' Notice: Most jurisdictions require at least a 30-day notice for month-to-month tenancies and up to 90 days for fixed-term lease renewals.
  • Keep the Tone Professional and Objective: Frame the increase around market data, rising operational costs, and property improvements rather than personal financial needs.
  • Include Clear Action Steps: Clearly outline how the tenant can accept the new terms, sign the renewal, or provide notice of their intent to vacate.

Understanding the Legal Framework Around Rent Increases

Before you sit down to draft your notice, you must understand the legal parameters governing rent hikes in your specific market. Landlords do not have unlimited authority to raise rents at any time or by any amount. Failing to comply with local housing laws can result in financial penalties, voided notices, or legal disputes with your tenants.

Notice Periods by Lease Type

The amount of warning you must give your tenant depends heavily on the type of lease agreement currently in place. Operating outside of these timelines can make your rent increase legally unenforceable.

Fixed-Term Leases

For a standard one-year or multi-year lease, you cannot raise the rent during the active lease term unless the lease agreement itself explicitly allows for a mid-term adjustment (which is highly unusual in residential agreements). You must wait until the lease is nearing its expiration date. The rent increase letter serves as an offer to renew the lease under new financial terms. Typically, this notice must be delivered 30, 60, or 90 days before the lease officially ends, depending on state law and the terms of the original contract.

Month-to-Month Tenancies

For periodic tenancies, such as month-to-month rental agreements, landlords have more flexibility regarding timing, but they must still adhere to strict statutory notice periods. In most states, a 30-day written notice is the minimum requirement. However, some states, such as California or Washington, require 60 or even 90 days' notice, particularly if the proposed increase exceeds a certain percentage of the current rent rate.

Rent Control and Rent Stabilization Laws

Many metropolitan areas and states have implemented rent control or rent stabilization measures that cap the maximum percentage a landlord can increase rent annually. For example, some jurisdictions tie maximum increases to the Consumer Price Index (CPI) plus a small fixed percentage.

Additionally, some states protect tenants from "de facto" evictions, where a landlord raises the rent by an exorbitant, unrealistic amount simply to force a tenant out. Ensure you review your local government's housing authority guidelines or consult a local real estate attorney to verify the legal cap in your municipality.

How to Determine the Right Rent Increase Amount

Setting the new rent amount requires a systematic approach. If you raise the rent too little, you fail to cover your rising operational expenses and lose out on potential yield. If you raise it too much, your tenant may choose to move out, leaving you with costly turnover expenses, cleaning bills, and vacancy periods that quickly wipe out any gains from the rent hike.

Conducting a Comparative Market Analysis (CMA)

To justify your rent increase to yourself and your tenant, you need to understand the current local market. Look at active listings of similar properties in your immediate neighborhood. Ensure you are comparing apples to apples by examining properties with the same:

  • Number of bedrooms and bathrooms
  • Square footage
  • Property type (e.g., condo, single-family home, duplex)
  • Amenities (e.g., in-unit laundry, parking, private yard, updated appliances)
  • Proximity to public transit, schools, and shopping centers

If comparable properties are renting for significantly more than your current rate, you have a strong market-based justification for an increase.

Calculating Your Operating Cost Increases

Your rent should also reflect the actual cost of holding and maintaining the asset. Review your financial records from the past 12 to 24 months. Look specifically for increases in:

  • Property taxes levied by your county or city
  • Homeowners insurance or landlord liability insurance premiums
  • HOA (Homeowners Association) dues
  • Utility rates (if you cover water, trash, or gas)
  • Contractor labor and material costs for routine maintenance

Essential Elements to Include in a Rent Increase Letter

A rent increase letter is a formal legal document. To ensure it is valid and leaves no room for misinterpretation, it must contain specific pieces of information. Missing even one of these details could cause confusion or render the notice void if challenged in a housing court.

1. Basic Identification Details

Start with the clear, unambiguous facts of the tenancy. This includes the full names of all adult tenants listed on the lease agreement, the complete property address (including unit or apartment number), and your contact information as the landlord or property manager.

2. Date of the Notice and Effective Date

Clearly state the date the letter is being written and delivered. This establishes the timeline for the notice period. Next, state the exact date the new rent rate will take effect. Make sure this effective date aligns perfectly with your local notice period requirements (e.g., if you deliver the letter on October 1st with a 30-day notice requirement, the new rent should take effect on November 1st or later).

3. The Current and New Financial Figures

Do not make the tenant do math. State the current rent amount, the amount of the increase (in both a dollar figure and a percentage), and the final new monthly rent amount. For example: "Your current rent is $1,500. The rent will increase by $75 (5%), making your new monthly rent $1,575."

4. Instructions for Acceptance or Vacating

You must provide the tenant with clear options and a deadline to respond. Typically, you should give them two choices:

  • Accept the new rent rate and sign a lease renewal or addendum by a specific deadline.
  • Reject the new rate and provide their formal notice of intent to vacate the property in accordance with the lease terms.

Provide a clear date by which they must return their signed agreement or their notice to vacate (usually 15 to 30 days before the new rent takes effect).

Choosing the Right Tone for Your Notice

The tone of your rent increase letter can make the difference between a cooperative tenant who accepts the increase and an angry tenant who stops communicating, damages the property, or leaves abruptly. The tone should be highly professional, polite, objective, and empathetic.

Avoid Emotional Explanations

Avoid sharing overly personal financial details, such as personal credit card debt, medical bills, or personal vacation plans. Tenants may view these reasons as unprofessional or selfish. Instead, focus on objective business realities. Frame the increase around keeping the property well-maintained, meeting market standards, and absorbing unavoidable operational cost increases like property taxes and municipal utilities.

Acknowledge and Value the Tenant

If the tenant has been reliable, pays on time, and takes good care of the property, say so. Express your gratitude for their tenancy. A simple sentence like, "We truly appreciate having you as a tenant and hope you choose to continue making this property your home," can soften the financial blow and preserve goodwill.

How to Write a Rent Increase Letter: Step-by-Step Guide

To help you structure your document correctly, here is a step-by-step breakdown of how to write a rent increase letter from scratch.

Step 1: Write a Clear Subject Line

If you are sending the notice via email (or attaching a PDF to an email), use a direct, professional subject line. Do not hide the purpose of the email. Use something like: "Notice of Rent Increase & Lease Renewal Offer - [Property Address]".

Step 2: Address the Tenant Formally

Begin with a professional greeting. Use the tenants' full names rather than informal nicknames. For example: "Dear John Doe and Jane Smith,".

Step 3: State the Core Message Immediately

Do not bury the lead. State in the very first paragraph that the rent is changing, the new amount, and the date it goes into effect. This establishes transparency from the outset.

Step 4: Explain the Reason for the Adjustment

In the second paragraph, briefly explain why the adjustment is necessary. Mention market trends, property tax increases, or recent improvements made to the building. Keep this section concise—one to three sentences is sufficient.

Step 5: Outline the Next Steps and Deadlines

Clearly explain what the tenant needs to do next. Give them a specific deadline to return the signed lease renewal or addendum. State who they should contact if they have any questions or wish to discuss the terms.

Step 6: Sign Off Professionally

End with a warm but professional closing, such as "Sincerely," or "Warm regards," followed by your printed name, signature, and phone number or email address.

Ready-to-Adapt Rent Increase Letter Templates

Below are two distinct templates you can copy, paste, and adapt for your specific rental properties. Be sure to fill in all the bracketed information before sending.

Template 1: For a Fixed-Term Lease Renewal

Use this template when a tenant's fixed-term lease is coming to an end and you want to offer them a renewal at a slightly higher rent rate.

[Date]

Tenant Name(s): [Tenant Full Names]
Property Address: [Full Rental Property Address, Unit Number]
Landlord/Manager Name: [Your Name or Company Name]
Contact Information: [Your Phone Number and Email]

Dear [Tenant Names],

We want to thank you for being valued tenants at [Property Address]. We appreciate your care of the property and your consistent, timely payments over the past year. We hope you have enjoyed your time in your home.

As your current lease term is scheduled to expire on [Lease End Date], we are writing to offer you a lease renewal. To keep pace with rising property taxes, insurance premiums, and local market rates, the monthly rent for the property will increase to $[New Rent Amount] per month, effective [Date New Rent Starts]. This represents an increase of $[Dollar Amount of Increase] (or [Percentage]% over your current rate).

All other terms and conditions of your original lease agreement will remain in full force and effect.

Please let us know your decision by [Response Deadline Date] so we can prepare the necessary paperwork. You have two options:

1. Accept the Lease Renewal: Please sign and return the enclosed lease addendum by [Response Deadline Date] to lock in this rate for another [12-month] term.
2. Decline the Renewal: If you choose not to renew, please submit your written [30/60-day] notice of intent to vacate by [Response Deadline Date].

If you have any questions or would like to discuss this notice, please do not hesitate to reach out to me directly at [Your Phone Number].

Thank you again for your residency.

Sincerely,

[Your Signature]
[Your Printed Name]
[Your Company Name, if applicable]

Template 2: For a Month-to-Month Agreement

Use this template when you are raising the rent for a tenant who is currently on a month-to-month rental agreement. Ensure you are providing the legally mandated notice period for your state.

[Date]

Tenant Name(s): [Tenant Full Names]
Property Address: [Full Rental Property Address, Unit Number]
Landlord/Manager Name: [Your Name or Company Name]
Contact Information: [Your Phone Number and Email]

Dear [Tenant Names],

This letter serves as formal written notice that the monthly rent for the property located at [Property Address] will be increased to $[New Rent Amount] per month. This change will take effect on [Date New Rent Starts, matching state notice requirements].

Your current monthly rent is $[Current Rent Amount]. This adjustment represents an increase of $[Dollar Amount of Increase] per month.

This adjustment is necessary due to ongoing increases in property operating costs, including municipal utility rates, property taxes, and property insurance over the past year. This change ensures we can continue to provide high-quality maintenance and management services for your home.

In accordance with your month-to-month rental agreement, please reply to this notice by [Response Deadline Date] to confirm your acceptance of these new terms. If you accept, you do not need to sign a new lease; your continued tenancy past [Date New Rent Starts] will constitute acceptance of the new rate.

If you do not wish to accept this rent adjustment and prefer to terminate your tenancy, you must provide written notice of your intent to vacate by [Response Deadline Date], in compliance with the [30-day] notice period required by your agreement.

Thank you for your cooperation and understanding.

Sincerely,

[Your Signature]
[Your Printed Name]
[Your Company Name, if applicable]

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced real estate investors make mistakes when communicating rent adjustments. Avoid these common pitfalls to keep your rental business running smoothly:

  • Failing to Check Local Rent Control Laws: Some jurisdictions limit annual rent hikes to a tiny percentage or tie them directly to local inflation rates. Increasing rent beyond these limits can result in heavy regulatory fines.
  • Providing Insufficient Notice: Sending a 30-day notice in a state that legally requires 60 days for a rent increase will invalidate the notice. You will have to restart the clock and lose out on a month of increased revenue.
  • Raising Rent in Retaliation or Discrimination: It is highly illegal to raise rent because a tenant complained to a housing inspector about repairs, joined a tenant union, or based on their protected class under the Fair Housing Act.
  • Not Getting the Agreement in Writing: Never agree to a rent increase over a casual phone call or text message. Always put the change in writing, send a formal letter, and obtain a signed lease addendum or digital signature to protect yourself legally.
  • Vague or Unclear Implementation Dates: Do not write "next month" or "soon." Use precise calendar dates (e.g., "January 1, 2026") to prevent any disputes regarding when the new payment is due.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much notice must a landlord give before raising the rent?

The notice period depends on state laws and your lease type. For month-to-month tenancies, most states require a minimum of 30 days' written notice, though some require 60 or 90 days. For fixed-term leases, you must typically notify the tenant 30 to 90 days before the lease expires, with the new rate taking effect once the new lease term begins.

Can a landlord raise the rent during an active fixed-term lease?

No, a landlord cannot raise the rent during a fixed-term lease unless the lease agreement contains a specific clause that allows for mid-term adjustments. In almost all standard residential leases, the rent rate is locked in for the entire duration of the lease term (usually one year).

What is a reasonable percentage to raise rent annually?

A typical annual rent increase ranges between 3% and 8%. This range usually covers inflation and standard increases in property taxes and insurance without causing excessive tenant turnover. However, you must always check local rent stabilization ordinances, which may cap annual increases at a much lower percentage.

Can a tenant negotiate a proposed rent increase?

Yes, tenants can—and often do—negotiate rent increases. If a tenant has been highly reliable, pays on time, and keeps the property in excellent condition, they may offer a counter-proposal (e.g., accepting a 3% increase instead of a 5% increase). Landlords often accept reasonable counter-offers to avoid the high costs associated with vacancy and tenant turnover.

What should a landlord do if a tenant refuses to pay the increased rent?

If a tenant refuses to pay the new rent rate after receiving proper legal notice, they are in violation of their lease terms. Landlords can initiate standard eviction proceedings for non-payment of rent, or choose not to renew the lease and issue a notice to vacate. Always consult a local landlord-tenant attorney before taking legal action.

Is a text message or verbal agreement a legally binding rent increase notice?

In almost all jurisdictions, verbal notices or casual text messages are not legally binding. Rent increase notices must be delivered in writing, either through a physical letter (delivered by hand or certified mail) or via a formal email with a digital signature option, depending on local state laws and lease provisions.

Writing a rent increase letter does not have to be a source of friction between you and your tenants. By understanding your local laws, establishing a fair market-based rate, and using a professional, structured template, you can successfully adjust your rental prices while maintaining a positive relationship with your tenants. Keep your records organized, plan your dates carefully, and always prioritize clear communication.
